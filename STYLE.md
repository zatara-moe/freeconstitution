# Free Constitution writing rules

These rules apply to every layer except the verbatim text, which is never edited.

## Who we write for
Young adults (16–25), neurodivergent readers (ADHD, autistic, dyslexic), English learners, and anyone reading on a phone under stress. Assume a smart reader with no legal background and limited time.

## Sentences
- One idea per sentence. Aim for 20 words or fewer; never more than 25.
- One point per paragraph, 3 sentences or fewer.
- Use "you" and active verbs. Write "Police need a warrant," not "A warrant is required."
- Use contractions sparingly in rights layers; plain is better than chatty.
- No idioms, sarcasm, metaphors, or jokes in any rights layer. ("Doing a lot of work," "the tip of the iceberg," "a slippery slope" — cut them.) Autistic and English-learner readers take them literally.
- Use numbers, not adjectives: "about half of states," "since 2014."
- Sentence case for headings. Never ALL CAPS for anything people must read.

## Terms
- Define a legal term in the same sentence the first time it appears on a page ("probable cause, meaning good reason to believe…").
- Glossary terms in `content/glossary.yml` are linked automatically.

## Accuracy (we have no lawyer review, so this is the review)
- State current law only when a Supreme Court decision or the text itself says it. Name the case and year once, in italics, and say what it decided in one sentence.
- If serious lawyers or judges disagree, say it is debated, give both readings fairly, and name the open question. Never pick a side.
- Separate law from opinion. "The Court held…" is fact. "Some argue… others argue…" goes in Who argued what or Actively contested.
- Never predict how a court will rule. Never tell a reader what to do in their specific case.
- Situation cards: say what the law generally is, note that states differ, and point to a lawyer or hotline.
- Quotes in Phrase by phrase must match the verbatim text exactly (the build checks this).
- When unsure a claim is right, leave it out.

## Neutrality
- Hot topics (guns, abortion, immigration, elections, religion, speech): describe what the text says, what the Court held, and what is still debated. No adjectives that signal approval or disapproval.
- "Who argued what" always shows at least two sides, in their own strongest terms.

## Hard history
- Before passages about slavery, the three-fifths clause, the Fugitive Slave Clause, "Indians not taxed," or exclusion from voting, add a one-sentence heads-up (`content_note`). Explain plainly. Never cut it from the verbatim text.

## Consistent labels (use exactly these)
The one-line version · Plain English · Show the original · Phrase by phrase · Picture it · What this means for you · Myth check · Go deeper · The scene · Back then · Words that changed · Who argued what · Key cases · Actively contested · Quick check · If this is happening to you

## Write the short version first
If you can't write the one-line version, the page isn't ready.
