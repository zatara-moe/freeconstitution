## Most rights protect every "person"

- The Constitution uses different words for different rights. The words matter.
- When it says "person," the right protects everyone in the U.S., whatever their immigration status.
- The Fifth and Fourteenth Amendments use "person." They protect your right to stay silent and to fair legal steps.
- In *Zadvydas v. Davis* (2001), the Supreme Court said due process applies to everyone in the U.S.

## Some rights are for citizens only

- Voting in federal and state elections is for citizens. Four amendments protect "the right of citizens" to vote.
- Serving in Congress and being President are for citizens.
- If you are not a citizen, do not register to vote, and never claim to be a citizen. Both can lead to deportation. See [First-time voter](/situations/first-time-voter/).

## Every child can go to public school

- Public schools cannot turn a child away because of the child's or parents' immigration status.
- In *Plyler v. Doe* (1982), the Supreme Court said this would break the Fourteenth Amendment's promise of equal protection.
- The ruling covers kindergarten through 12th grade. It does not cover college.

## Still being decided

- Some rights belong to "the people," including the Fourth Amendment's protection from unreasonable searches.
- In *United States v. Verdugo-Urquidez* (1990), the Court said "the people" means people with enough connection to the United States.
- Lower courts have disagreed about how that applies to people in the U.S. without legal status.
- Courts generally apply the protection of the home and the right to remain silent to everyone in the U.S.
- In *Department of Homeland Security v. Thuraissigiam* (2020), the Court said people stopped soon after crossing the border can get fewer protections in some cases.
