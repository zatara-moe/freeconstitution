## Verbatim

**Section 1.** The right of citizens of the United States, who are eighteen years of age or older, to vote shall not be denied or abridged by the United States or by any State on account of age.

**Section 2.** The Congress shall have power to enforce this article by appropriate legislation.

## Plain English

**Section 1.** The right of U.S. citizens age 18 or older to vote cannot be denied or limited by the federal government or any state because of age.

**Section 2.** Congress has the power to enforce this amendment by passing appropriate laws.

## What this means for you

### Voting at 18

The Twenty-Sixth Amendment set 18 as the voting age in every election: federal, state, and local. Before 1971, most states required voters to be 21. No government can now set a voting age higher than 18.

### Younger voters in some places

The amendment does not stop states or cities from letting younger people vote. A few cities let 16- and 17-year-olds vote in city elections. Some states let 17-year-olds vote in a primary if they will be 18 by the general election.

### Registering and voting

Registration deadlines, ID rules, and polling places are set by state law and differ widely. Many states let you register before you turn 18. Your state's election website or vote.gov can show you the rules.

### If you are turned away

If you are 18 or older and told you cannot vote because of your age, ask to speak with an election official. You may be able to cast a provisional ballot, meaning a ballot counted later once your eligibility is checked. You can also call the Election Protection hotline at 866-687-8683.

## About

The Twenty-Sixth Amendment was ratified on July 1, 1971. Its ratification was the fastest of any amendment, about three months and one week after Congress passed it.

The Vietnam War drove the change. Young men could be drafted at 18 but could not vote in most states until 21. The slogan "Old enough to fight, old enough to vote" summed up the argument.

The direct cause was *Oregon v. Mitchell* (1970). The Court held that Congress could set the voting age at 18 for federal elections, but not for state and local elections. That would have meant 18-year-olds could vote for President but not for governor in many states. The amendment set 18 as the voting age for all elections.
