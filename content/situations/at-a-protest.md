## Where and how you can protest

- Public sidewalks, parks, and plazas are generally protected places to protest.
- Private property is not. The owner can ask you to leave.
- Cities can require permits for marches that block streets.
- Cities can set rules about time, place, and manner. These rules cannot be based on what you are saying.

## What is not protected

- Blocking traffic without a permit.
- Trespassing on private property.
- Damaging property.
- Threats of violence.

These are not protected, even when they happen during a protest.

## Recording at a protest

Many federal appeals courts have said you have a right to record police in public. Do not get in their way while you record. See the card "Recording police" for more.

## If police order the crowd to leave

- An order to leave is called a dispersal order.
- Staying after a dispersal order can be a separate crime.
- Leave by the route police give, if they give one.

## If you are arrested

- Do not physically resist, even if you think the arrest is unfair. A lawyer can challenge it later.
- Say: "I want to remain silent. I want a lawyer." Then stop talking.
- Say: "I do not consent to a search." Police generally need a warrant to search your phone.
- Many states let you make a phone call after you are booked. Call a lawyer or someone who can find you one.

## The law you are citing

**First Amendment (1791):** The government cannot punish you for speaking or for gathering peacefully.

> Congress shall make no law... abridging the freedom of speech, or of the press; or the right of the people peaceably to assemble, and to petition the Government for a redress of grievances.

**Fourth Amendment (1791):** Police generally need a warrant to search you or your things.

**Fifth Amendment (1791):** You do not have to be a witness against yourself.
