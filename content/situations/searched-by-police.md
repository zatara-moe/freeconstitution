## Why saying "no" matters

Saying "I do not consent" out loud protects your rights later in court. It matters even if the officer searches anyway. Do not physically resist. A lawyer can challenge the search later.

## When police do not need a warrant

A warrant is a court order signed by a judge. Police generally need one to search you or your things. There are exceptions. Police can search without a warrant if:

- **You agree.** This is why "I do not consent" matters.
- **They arrest you.** They can search you and the area within your reach.
- **There is an emergency.** For example, a fire, someone screaming for help, or evidence about to be destroyed.
- **Something illegal is in plain view.** They must see it from a place they are allowed to be.
- **They stop you with reasonable suspicion.** Reasonable suspicion means a specific reason to think you are involved in a crime. They can pat down your outer clothes for weapons. This is called a "frisk."
- **Your car is involved.** Police may have probable cause, meaning good reason to believe there is evidence in your car. Then they can often search it without a warrant.

During a frisk, police generally cannot go into your pockets. The exception is if they feel something that is clearly a weapon or clearly illegal.

## At your home

- Police generally need a warrant to enter your home.
- You do not have to open the door. You can talk through it.
- If they say they have a warrant, ask them to slide it under the door or hold it to a window.
- Read it. It should be signed by a judge. It should list your address and what they are looking for.
- If they have no warrant, say: "I do not consent to you entering my home."

## On the street

- Ask: "Am I being detained, or am I free to leave?"
- If you are free to leave, leave calmly.
- If you are detained, you may have to give your name in some states. You do not have to answer other questions.
- You do not have to agree to a search of your bag or pockets. Say: "I do not consent to this search."

## In your car

- Pull over safely. Turn on the inside light at night. Keep your hands on the steering wheel.
- Show your driver's license, registration, and proof of insurance if asked.
- You do not have to answer other questions. Say: "I want to remain silent."
- Say: "I do not consent to a search of this vehicle."
- Police may still search if they have probable cause. Your "no" still protects your rights in court.

## With your phone

- Police generally need a warrant to search your phone, even after an arrest. The Supreme Court decided this in *Riley v. California* (2014).
- Say: "I do not consent to a search of my phone."
- You do not have to tell police your passcode because they ask.
- Courts disagree about whether a judge can order you to unlock a phone. The rules for fingerprint and face unlock are also unsettled and differ by state.

## The law you are citing

**Fourth Amendment (1791):** The government cannot search you or take your things unreasonably. Police generally need a warrant signed by a judge.

> The right of the people to be secure in their persons, houses, papers, and effects, against unreasonable searches and seizures, shall not be violated

**Fifth Amendment (1791):** The government cannot force you to be a witness against yourself in a criminal case.

> nor shall be compelled in any criminal case to be a witness against himself
